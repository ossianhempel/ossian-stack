# Headlines

The headline is the ad. Four out of five readers read the headline and skip
the rest, so make a complete statement in it. Never publish the first headline
you write.

## The three questions

A headline must make the reader understand three things at the same time:

1. **What is this about?**
2. **Is this for me?**
3. **What is the PROMISE** - the problem solved or solution offered - and how
   confident am I that you'll deliver it?

Together these are the **Curiosity Gap**: tell the reader what it's about, who
it's for, and what it promises, without revealing the answer.

`"The Big Idea"` fails all three - grammatically clear, informationally empty.
That failure is invisible to "did I say what I meant?", because the writer did
say what they meant.

## Position matters more than length

A scrolling reader reads only the **beginning and the end** of a headline. The
first two or three words say what this thing is; the final words carry the
PROMISE; the middle is skimmed past in milliseconds.

The failure this catches: a headline whose identifying words sit behind a
preamble - *"A Few Thoughts On…"*, *"The Reason Why…"*, *"Some Notes About…"*
- is perfectly clear as a sentence and unreadable at scroll speed.

**Fix:** delete leading words until the first two or three name the thing, and
move the outcome to the end.

Leading with a number works for two reasons: it is declarative, which implies
conviction, and it *sounds short* - `"The 1 Thing…"` reads as a low barrier to
entry.

## The rewriting process

Run all four steps. The output of step 1 is supposed to be too long.

1. **Say it in full**, however many words it takes.
   *"The 8 Things You Should Do On A Daily Basis In Order To Become The Best
   Person You Can Possibly Become, No Matter What Obstacles Are In Your Way."*
2. **Maximize the PROMISE.** Can you promise more? What deeper emotional want
   sits behind this problem?
   *"…In Order To Get More Done, Achieve Your Goals, And Start Living Your
   Best Life."*
3. **Replace vague words with the reader's specific interests**, so their eyes
   latch onto two or three while skimming.
   *"…To Be More Productive, Achieve Success, And Gain True Financial
   Freedom."*
4. **Cut every connecting word you can** - *if, when, does, it, too, for*.
   *"8 Daily Habits That Will Make You More Productive, Achieve Success, And
   Gain True Financial Freedom."*

**The loop has a reject.** If step 4 doesn't produce something powerful, start
over at step 1 - or don't write the piece. Revision here is a filter on which
pieces exist, not a polish on pieces already committed to.

Note the direction: find the promise at full length *first*, then let
compression take connectors instead of meaning.

## Escalating the PROMISE without becoming clickbait

More emotional PROMISE means more clicks, comments and shares, because it
speaks to the reader's identity:

- *"…Become Rich"* → *"…Become So Rich You Never Have To Think About Money
  Again"*
- *"…Not Lose Your Job"* → *"…Not Get Fired On Your First Day"*

Both members of each pair are equally clear. The winner is not the clearer
one. Clarity decides whether it's legible; emotion decides whether it's worth
the click. Two separate passes on the same sentence.

**The constraint that separates this from clickbait:** approved rewrites get
more compelling by *adding* information. Clickbait gets more compelling by
*withholding* it.

Rejected: *"You'll Never Believe What Happened Next!"* / *"The Secret No One
Wants You To Know..."*

**Checkable rule: if the line got more compelling while saying less, it
failed.**

## Proven formats

- **Big Numbers** - concrete, definitive, unexpectedly large.
  *"3,000 People Just Filed For Unemployment In This Small Town In Arkansas.
  Here's Why."*
- **Dollar Signs** - money is universal, widens the curiosity gap.
  *"$400 Million Is How Much You Need To Make In Order To Afford This Insane
  Mansion In Malibu."*
- **Credible Names** - the recognizable name does the hooking.
  *"Will Smith's Advice On How To Live A Fulfilling Life Will Change The Way
  You See The World Forever."*
- **"This Just Happened"** - *Just, Recently, Today, Now* signal priority.
  *"Michael Jordan Just Gave A Press Conference And NBA Executives Are
  Furious."*
- **The Success Story** - a rare outcome, summarized.
  *"How This Small Team Managed To Secure A Six-Figure Investment In Less Than
  1 Week."*
- **Things That Shouldn't Go Together** - unexpected combinations force a
  pause. *"7 Things KFC And Miley Cyrus Have In Common."*
- **For The Industry** - name the audience in the title.
  *"3 Things All Successful Small Business Owners Do To Stay Profitable."*
- **The Topic Within The Topic** - a parenthetical layered onto any format.
  *"7 Ways The Real Estate Industry Is Changing (And How You Should Be
  Investing Your Money)."*
- **Two puzzle pieces** - broad main title plus ultra-specific subtitle.
  *"Atomic Habits: An Easy & Proven Way To Build Good Habits & Break Bad
  Ones."*
- **"How To"** - when stuck, type *How to* and finish the sentence. It is
  always at least serviceable.

## POWER phrases

Swap generic quantifiers for words that signal urgency, stakes, or that this
piece differs from every other article on the topic.

*"7 Ways…"* → *"7 Little-Known Ways"* / *"7 Small But Powerful Ways"*

Live examples: *Crucial*, *Unforgettable*, *Memorable*, *Eye-Opening*,
*Painful*, *Emerging*, *Quietly*.

## Additional rules

- **Clear first, clever second.** True creativity is the art of clarity.
- **Be specific** with numbers, minutes, days, percentages.
- **Address a specific emotion** - fear of missing out, fear of failure,
  wanting results, wanting to be liked.
- **Make it searchable.** Would the reader type this into Google? If yes, good
  headline.
- **Place the reader inside it.** *"14 Kinds of Pumpkins"* is boring;
  *"7 Kinds of Pumpkins You Can Grow on a City Balcony"* is not; *"The
  10-Minute Guide to Growing Giant Pumpkins on a City Balcony"* is a double
  benefit.
- **Distribution before creation.** MrBeast staffs headline writers; if the
  headline is good, they make the video. A retitle from *"How Writing Online
  Changed My Life"* to *"How Writing Online Made Me a Millionaire"* flipped a
  bottom-decile video overnight.
- **Even a tweet has a headline.** Ask what the headline of this post would
  be.

## Specificity operates on two objects

Do not confuse them:

- **Headline-level specificity** decides whether *this* reader clicks *this*
  piece.
- **Category-level specificity** decides which shelf they file you on - the
  question being answered is "if this is the category I care about, who is the
  #1 writer here?"

A run of hyper-specific headlines across scattered categories passes the first
and fails the second, leaving the reader no box to put you in.

## Subheads are headlines one level down

Every rule above applies to every bolded Main Point inside the piece. A subhead
that is a single word or a bare label - *"POWER phrases"*, *"The rewriting
process"* - withholds the answer and forces the reader to stop and read the
paragraph to find out what the point was.

**Bar:** a reader who skims nothing but the bolded subheads should feel they
got it.

**Fix:** write the subhead as the finding. *"POWER phrases"* becomes *"Swap
generic quantifiers for words that signal urgency."*
