# Introductions

The click only buys a hearing. From the first word the writing is racing a
dwindling attention span, and every second is another chance for the reader to
ask "why am I still here?"

## The job: hook, don't summarize

School taught that an introduction describes the importance of the topic and
summarizes the key points. Ignore that. The objective is to hook the reader
into reading more. A great intro buys goodwill the same way an electrifying
opening scene does.

## The three questions, again

The introduction answers the same three questions the headline did - what is
this about, is this for me, what is the PROMISE. The headline answering them
is not enough.

**Revision test:** cover the headline and read the intro alone. Can it still
answer all three? Whatever the headline's PROMISE was maximized to is what the
first five or so sentences must visibly start paying, or the maximization
reads as bait.

## What a hook is

A hook is any **half-told story**:

| Type | Move |
|---|---|
| **Question** | Pose an intriguing question, don't answer it |
| **Narrative** | Start a story, withhold the conclusion |
| **Discovery** | Reveal part of a finding |
| **Argument** | State the case, not how you got there |

Hooks tease your best talking points. They trigger the storytelling machinery
the reader already has.

### Generating hooks

1. Ask: *"if someone else wrote my intro, what are the most captivating
   questions they could pose to make me excited to read this?"*
2. Write the questions down, even ones you can't answer.
3. Rank them by how much they interest *you*.
4. The top ones become your hooks. Pose them; don't reveal the answers.

You and your audience evolved the same machinery, so questions that hook you
hook most of them.

## Start at the height of the story

The highest-engagement openings begin at the peak, not the setup:

> "The first time I became a millionaire, I was living in my parents'
> basement."

The reader is inside the story before deciding whether they wanted to be.

**Rule: if your story gets better four paragraphs later, delete your first
four paragraphs.** If a piece depends on the reader surviving the first few
pages, it doesn't need those pages.

## The greased chute

Open with a punchy, curiosity-building sentence, rarely longer than eight
words. It starts the reader sliding down the page.

- *"You've finally found it."*
- *"Here's the big misconception…"*
- *"Stop me if you've heard this before."*

A weak first line kills the post no matter how good the body is. Every opener
should do one of four things: create a curiosity gap, make a bold claim, drop
a specific number that stops the scroll, or challenge a belief the reader
currently holds.

## The structures

Every valid structure **opens on a single sentence and closes on a single
sentence.** That part is invariant. The middle is a variable that the density
of the point selects.

```
1/3/1          1/5/1          1/3/2/1        1/5/2/1
1/4/1/1        1/3/1 + 1/3/1  1/3/Subhead/3/1
1/3/1 + Bulleted List         1 + Subhead
```

There is nothing to memorize here. These nine are the output space of two
moves applied repeatedly: **extend the middle** (one point needs more
description) or **brace a second chunk** (there is a second point). If your
draft lands outside the nine, what broke is the bookends.

### 1/3/1 is the default

One opener, three description sentences, one conclusion.

> *This first sentence is your opener. This second sentence clarifies your
> opener. This third sentence reinforces the point with some credibility or
> amplified description. This fourth sentence rounds out your argument,
> guiding the reader toward your conclusion. This fifth sentence is your
> strong conclusion.*

Two independent reasons it works:

1. **Shape is read before words are.** A wall of text is rejected on a signal
   no amount of clarity can reach. 1/3/1 tells the reader you aren't going to
   make them suffer.
2. **A visible terminus makes effort affordable.** When readers can "see" the
   end, they're more likely to put in the effort to get there - which is why
   the last sentence has to exist before the middle is trusted to run long.

### It's a ceiling, not a quota

A fixed sentence count invites padding: three middle sentences get filled
whether or not there are three ideas. Read it as a range with an upper bound.

**1/7/1 or 1/8/1 territory means one of exactly two things** - too many points
at once, or unnecessary description. Both are cuts, but different cuts: one
removes ideas from the paragraph, the other removes words from the ideas.

The escape hatch: when a point genuinely needs seven sentences, keep it as one
block braced by single-sentence bookends rather than splitting it into two
padded paragraphs.

## Counter the five skepticisms

Skepticism often outweighs the hook, and the reader quits early. Counter all
five inside the intro:

| Skepticism | Reader thinks | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| **Superficial** | "You won't tell me anything I don't know" | Tease your original insights |
| **Irrelevant** | "You won't cover what I care about" | List the points you'll cover |
| **Sloppy** | "I don't want more bad writing" | Rewrite: clear, succinct, intriguing |
| **Implausible** | "You won't answer your own hooks" | Keep hooks realistic; don't oversell |
| **Untrustworthy** | "You're not qualified" | Share credentials, or make the hooks undeniable |

Countering *irrelevant* by listing the points requires the skeleton to already
exist - which is why the skeleton comes before the final intro.

## Write it twice, and skeleton in between

1. Sketch a working intro for yourself. It tells you the purpose of what
   you're writing.
2. Skeleton the Main Points. Under a fixed word budget, every sentence the
   intro spends is a sentence a Main Point loses.
3. Rewrite the intro against the skeleton, for the reader.

## Keep it short and skimmable

- Make the point fast. Anything not absolutely necessary, delete it.
- Use subheads to chop a long intro into digestible bites. Readers' eyes seek
  subheads before paragraphs, and a subhead shows the first milestone is only
  a few sentences away. `1/3/Subhead/3/1` puts one *inside* the intro.
- A 1/3/1 or 1/5/1 opener is easy to read. A seven-sentence opening paragraph
  is not.

## Get feedback here, not on the draft

Ask a reader: *"after reading my intro, do you want to keep reading?"*

- **No** - you just saved yourself writing an article nobody cares about.
- **Yes** - you have confidence the perspective is interesting.

The best time to ask for feedback is after the introduction, not after the
first draft.
