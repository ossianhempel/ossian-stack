# Forms, Ideas, and What to Write About

## The three-step process

Everything written on the internet follows this. Fix all three before drafting.

### Step 1 - What *type* of writing is this?

| Form | What it is | Mental goal |
|---|---|---|
| **Actionable Guide** | How to do a specific thing | Get bookmarked |
| **Opinion** | A stance, argued | Get agreed with or argued with |
| **Curated List** | A collection of things worth knowing | Get saved and shared |
| **Story** | A narrative that carries a lesson | Get felt |
| **Credible Talking Head** | Expert commentary on a development | Get quoted |

People read publications for three reasons: opinions, news, or to learn how to
do something. That's it.

**Habit worth building:** every time you read anything online, pause and ask
"what type of writing is this?"

### Step 2 - What *idea* am I communicating?

1. **Explanation** - when/where/how/what/why something happens
2. **Habits** - to achieve a destination, goal, or state of being
3. **Mistakes** - keeping you from achieving it
4. **Lessons** - learned in pursuit of it
5. **Tips** - that help in your own pursuit of it
6. **Stories** - that symbolize or explain some aspect of it

### Step 3 - Who is this for?

One named reader, as narrow as you can stand. The broader you are, the more
confusing you are. Specificity forces a choice: the reader either shouts "HELL
YES" or walks away - and that is the point. A box is what you want, because it
tells readers where to file you on the bookshelf in their mind.

## The form-mismatch failure

The most common structural failure in online writing, and it is invisible to
sentence-level revision.

A writer titles a piece *"How To Land Your First Client As A Freelance
Photographer"* - an Actionable Guide - then writes a bit about clients, a bit
about lenses, a bit about accounting, a bit about editing. That is a Curated
List. Every sentence can be clear and the piece still fails, because the form
promised and the form delivered don't match. The reader leaves confused and
unfulfilled.

**The fix is to split, not to write longer.**

Three questions to run before drafting an Actionable Guide:

1. What type of writing is this?
2. How do I make this the ULTIMATE guide to this topic - so specific, so
   comprehensive, that the reader bookmarks it, shares it, and feels no need
   to read another guide on the same subject?
3. **Can I make this more specific? Am I cramming too much into one article?**
   Creating the single best resource does not require writing a book.

"Would they bookmark it?" is a checkable quality bar, because bookmarking
implies the reader expects to come back.

## The Golden Intersection

> Answering the reader's question × telling them an entertaining story

Whenever you talk about yourself, it must give necessary context to the
reader. Writing about how to learn a skill? Also tell them how *you* learned
it. Writing about falling out of love? Tell them about a time you did.

**The failure mode is making the product the main character.** Instead of
writing about your home-farming product, write about the mistakes people make
starting a farm at home - and then tell readers you made those same mistakes
early on.

The tell that you got this wrong is the PR construction **"Enter: <Company
Name>"**.

## Credibility and the CTA

Self-promotion should never feel like self-promotion. It should feel like
context.

**Do not put the call to action in the signature.** Embed the link inside a
relevant Main Point, so the CTA and the credibility statement are the same
sentence:

> "I have been helping people build their personal brands online for more than
> five years now, and more than 10,000 people have taken my free personal
> branding email course (link)."

A bare credential repels on its own. The credibility claim and the offer to
help the reader get the same result are load-bearing for each other, which is
why merging them is what makes either survive.

Never give the CTA the spotlight - very little is gained by hard-selling
readers on anything.

## The three content buckets

Every writer should be publishing into all three.

| Bucket | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **General Audience** | Universal topics - habits, life lessons, productivity - approached through your own specific lens. Not "time management," but "time management techniques I use as a solo iOS developer." |
| **Niche Audience** | Hyper-relevant to your expertise. Speak to the intimate pain points of the target reader. Optionally still using universal topics to broaden reach - do both. |
| **Company / Industry** | The environment you exist within. A violinist writes about the violin industry; a SaaS founder about SaaS. Easiest to pinpoint, and required to be *seen* as a leader in the category. |

## Volume, and what deserves the volume

> Writers who succeed on the internet are the ones with the largest libraries
> of content. In the game of Online Writing, volume wins.

Two consequences:

- **A quality bar that can't be sustained across hundreds of pieces is the
  wrong bar.** The per-piece checklist has to be cheap enough to run every
  time.
- **Timely content is a double-edged sword.** A piece about an announcement
  from seven years ago can't be re-published or re-shared, so its whole return
  is collected on day one. Evergreen pieces keep earning through re-shares,
  which makes them the only kind worth polishing past the checklist.

## The measurement window

The first six months on the internet should be spent exploring, practicing,
and gathering data. You cannot tell whether you have something to say - or
whether a platform is worth it - on less than six months of consistent
publishing.

That window only produces usable data if the library grows fast enough inside
it, and only if the variables are controlled: hold the headline format or the
intro structure constant between pieces, change one thing, and double down on
whatever attracted viewership.

Publish before the book, before the product, before "positioning yourself."
Publishing first reduces risk, reveals your voice through data, builds the
audience from day one, and tells you what people actually want. Mark Manson
used blog data to pick the title, structure, and concept of *The Subtle Art of
Not Giving A F\*ck*.
