# Main Points, Rhythm, and Endings

## Write backwards

The sweet spot of an online article is **800 to 1,200 words**. The job is to
pack maximum value into the Main Points *without* inflating the count. So the
real question is how much **real estate** each point gets.

Skeleton the Main Points before - or immediately after - the working intro.
Having more Main Points does not mean making the piece longer. It means each
point gets less.

## Depth is set by point count

| Main Points | Template | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| 1-3 | **1/2/5/3/1** - the twelve-sentence form | ~200 words each |
| 4 | **1/3/1** - five sentences | ~85 words each |
| 5+ | The Main Point + 1-3 sentences | ~40 words each |

Three deep points already consume most of a 1,200-word article. Picking the
deep template for a seven-point piece is what produces the bloat it was meant
to prevent.

The shallow end has its own justification: in a five-plus-point piece, the
reader is there for the Main Points themselves - not a dissertation on each.

### The twelve-sentence template (1/2/5/3/1)

```
1.  Opener
2.  Clarify the opener
3.  Why the reader should care
4.  Start expanding on the point
5.  A story, or a credible piece of insight
6.  Build on it - tell them something they didn't know
7.  A small conclusion
8.  Why that conclusion matters
9.  Recap what you just told the reader
10. Reinforce with an additional tidbit or insight
11. Drive the point home
12. Remind the reader of the important takeaway
```

Note what this is: the whole article in miniature. Sentences 1-3 are an
introduction, the middle is proof, 9-12 are a conclusion.

### The five-sentence template (1/3/1)

```
1. Opener
2. Clarifier
3. Why the reader should care
4. Mini conclusion
5. Why that conclusion matters specifically to the target reader
```

## Main Points are statements, not labels

A Main Point is a **statement**. Single-word subheads are the failure case -
they withhold the answer and force the reader into the paragraph to find out
what the point was.

**Bar:** a reader skimming nothing but the bolded subheads must still feel
they got it.

This constraint is what makes the shallow template survivable. If the Main
Point itself carries the answer, a reader who skips the supporting sentences
still receives the PROMISE. If the Main Point is a label, the shallow template
collapses into nothing.

Point count, depth, and phrasing are one decision, not three: the more points,
the more load the subhead itself must carry.

## Parallel phrasing is a credibility signal

Repetition across subheads tells the reader the piece was deliberately
organized, before any claim in it is evaluated:

- *"Smart People X" / "Smart People Y" / "Smart People Z"*
- *"The Art Of Writing Introductions" / "…Main Points" / "…Conclusions"*

**Draft the Main Points together as a set and read them as a list in
isolation.** Parallelism is invisible while writing them one at a time.

## The chunk test

For each section, ask: **what is the one singular point I'm driving home
here?** Then of the next one: **does it really warrant its own section?**

These two tests run in opposite directions on the same boundary:

- Sentence count catches the chunk that should have been **split**.
- "Does it warrant its own section" catches the chunk that was split when it
  should have been **cut** - two short paragraphs both passing a length
  ceiling while making one point between them.

The subhead makes this enforceable rather than introspective: a subhead *is*
the single point of the chunk beneath it, so failing to write one is the
diagnosis that the chunk has no single point.

## Rhythm

Every test above is local, and all of them pass cleanly on a page that is
monotonous. Sameness is invisible to any check run one paragraph at a time.

**Never three or more long paragraphs in a row.** The single-sentence brace
stops working when repeated - you can spend the braced-long-paragraph move
roughly twice before the reader stops registering it as a resolution.

**Vary internal rhythm too.** Make consecutive long paragraphs *sound*
different: one built from short declarative sentences, the next from one long
winding sentence. Place single sentences before and after each.

> There's something about reading a single sentence after a long paragraph
> that gives a reader the same feeling a listener gets hearing a chord resolve
> on the piano.

### Crescendo and decrescendo

`1/1/1+` - several single-sentence paragraphs in a row, each restating or
reinforcing the last:

> Two. Years.
>
> Not 3 days.
>
> Not a few weeks.

Follow it deliberately with a **longer** paragraph. After a few big steps the
reader doesn't want to run anymore - they want to settle into the thing you're
talking about. Crescendo, decrescendo, repeat.

**This reverses every ceiling above.** Cut, split, shorten - taken alone those
push a draft toward uniform short paragraphs, which is exactly the monotony
that loses readers. A long paragraph can be an earned reward.

**So check what precedes a block before diagnosing it.** A seven-sentence
paragraph after a `1/1/1+` run is the decrescendo doing its job, not a point
that needed splitting.

## Endings

**Conclusions are optional.** The last Main Point is technically the climax,
and the PROMISE was already discharged by the points themselves. In an
800-1,200 word article a conclusion should take a paragraph or a single
sentence.

Because each Main Point already closes itself, a separate conclusion section
is the fourth recap the reader has been handed.

Three endings, instead of a section:

1. **In-structure final point** - write the last Main Point to stay inside the
   same structure as the others while sounding like a close. *Default.*
2. **Extended Final Main Point** - bake the mini conclusion into the last
   point as one extra paragraph.
3. **The Summary** - a bulleted recap of the Main Points plus one
   all-encompassing sentence. Reserve for actionable guides and very long
   pieces - which is also the case where the deep per-point template was
   already abandoned.

## Clarity standards that run underneath all of this

- **Rate of Revelation.** Every sentence advances the story or reveals
  something new. Anything not absolutely necessary, delete it.
- **Say what you meant.** If a sentence appears unclear, ask what you meant to
  say, and rewrite. Repeat.
- **Stopping rule: unsummarizable.** You're done when removing any words would
  cost an idea. That is Rate of Revelation applied per word rather than per
  sentence.
- **Two extra questions on each revision pass:** could this be more specific?
  Does every sentence earn the next one?

## Length ceiling as a router

Paragraph length is a cheap detector that tells you *which* pass to run:

```
Paragraph > ~5 sentences
├── preceded by a 1/1/1+ run? → intentional decrescendo, leave it
├── too many points?          → split (or split the whole piece)
└── too much description?     → cut words, not ideas
```
