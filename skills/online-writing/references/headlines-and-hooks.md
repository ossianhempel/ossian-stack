# Headlines and hooks

Read for Packaging and for working headlines in Topic mode. The goal is an
honest, specific reason for this reader to start, followed immediately by a
reason to continue.

## Find the strongest material

Summarize the reader, the piece's actual payoff, and the evidence you can use.
Look for a concrete mistake, surprising result, consequential decision, lived
scene, useful mechanism, or tension between what the reader expects and what
the writer found. If none is present, wording alone will have limited value.

Choose among approaches the material supports:

| Approach | Source of interest | Required support |
|---|---|---|
| Specific outcome | A result the reader wants, with a clear scope or constraint | The body explains how or why that outcome is achievable |
| Costly mistake | A recognizable action with an unexpected consequence | A real mistake and a supported consequence; no inflated loss |
| Expectation versus observation | What happened differs from the reader's likely expectation | Evidence of the difference, not a contrarian assertion |
| Scene or decision | The reader enters at a meaningful moment | A real event, relevant detail, and a development worth following |
| Mechanism | A concrete explanation changes how the reader understands a problem | The body demonstrates the explanation |
| Reader question | A precise question the reader already wants answered | A worthwhile answer; avoid questions with obvious answers |

These are choices, not templates to fill or a quota of one per approach. The
final options must differ in what creates interest, not just their syntax.
Draft competing candidates for promising angles before selecting the final
set. A factually accurate topic description is not enough: ask what makes this
reader want to know what happens, why it happens, or what they can do about it.

## Title, promise, and curiosity

A headline communicates subject, audience, and promise together. Not all need
to be spelled out: a recognizable situation can identify the audience. A main
title can create intrigue while a subtitle provides specificity.

Make the promised result or avoided consequence imaginable. A headline about
"better productivity" is weak until the reader knows what changes in their day.
Use the piece's real details to do that work. A number can clarify a finite
scope, but use one only when the body actually supplies that number of things.
Put the strongest specific subject near the beginning and the payoff or tension
near the end. Remove introductory phrases that delay either. Treat emotion as
the reader's response to recognizable stakes, not an adjective to insert.

Leave an interesting question unanswered while making the subject clear. A
reader should know what they are choosing to read, not be tricked into clicking.
Give enough detail that they can recognize why this is relevant to them.

Check fulfillment: if the headline promises a method, the body cannot stop at
an opinion. If it promises an outcome, the evidence cannot establish only a
possibility. If it implies a universal rule, an anecdote alone cannot support it.
Reduce the claim or name the missing support; never quietly expand the body.
Bound an anecdote through an accurate title and scope, such as making clear it
describes the writer's experience. Don't overload every opening with caveats
when it makes no general claim; place methodological limits where the body
begins interpreting the result. Accuracy should sharpen the promise, not turn
the hook into a disclaimer.

## Opening and continued attention

The title earns entry; the opening begins delivering. Use the strongest relevant
sentence or scene, including material currently buried later in the piece.
Cut setup when the reader can understand the event or claim without it.

An opening sequence can move from **specific situation → tension or consequence
→ reason to keep reading**. It should not summarize the entire article or repeat
the title in different words. A useful claim can be revealed early while leaving
its explanation, application, or resolution for the body; curiosity does not
require withholding every answer.

Every line should advance the reader's understanding or experience. Short
sentences are not enough if they say the same thing. For an article, a 1/3/1 or
1/5/1 opening can make the destination visible. For a feed post, first-visible
lines must make sense without relying on the hidden continuation. Inspect the
rendering when possible; otherwise disclose that this part is unverified.

## A worked contrast

Suppose the material says: the writer asked an agent to fix a failing test,
watched each command and dictated each file edit, then realized they were still
doing the debugging. On a later task, they described the failing behavior, the
constraints, and the checks required; the agent returned a patch for review.
There are no measured time savings or quality scores.

Weak headline: **How to Unlock the Power of AI Agents**

Weak hook: **AI agents are changing how we work. But are you using them correctly?**

Neither names the specific behavior, tension, or payoff. Stronger alternatives
can use different supported angles:

- **Recognition:** "If you dictate every edit, you're still doing the debugging."
  Opening: "I asked an agent to fix a failing test. Then I told it which file to
  open, what to change, and which command to run."
- **Mechanism:** "What I gave my coding agent instead of another command."
  Opening: "On my next task, I described what was broken and how I would verify
  the fix. I left the choice of edits to the agent."
- **Personal discovery:** "I asked a coding agent to debug and kept choosing its steps."
  Opening: "The agent was typing. I was making every debugging decision."

These are illustrations of specificity and different angles, not stock language
to reuse. They make no unsupported claim about speed, accuracy, or autonomy.
Whether each fits depends on the actual writer's voice and the body that follows.

## Select before presenting

Reject a candidate if it could describe almost any article on the broad topic,
adds facts absent from the material, inflates the stakes, relies on a canned
teaser, or promises something the body doesn't deliver. Check each title/opening
pair for a repeated first idea. Read it aloud against the writer's samples.

For each surviving candidate, explain the specific reader appeal and supporting
detail. Name the question it makes the reader want answered, or the concrete
benefit that makes a direct headline useful. A chronological description or a
familiar instruction is not compelling just because it is true. Prefer a
supported tension or discovery to an ordinary procedure, and choose among the
strongest options rather than padding the set with weaker ones.

Recommend the strongest fit for the writer's purpose, noting the tradeoff
such as clarity versus intrigue or narrow relevance versus broader recognition.
Don't label it viral or proven without outcome evidence. If results are available,
use comparable posts to refine the choice; otherwise treat it as an experiment.
